Why these variants look the way they do

Five token sets over one stylesheet. Not a single layout rule differs between them — only colour, type, size, measure and spacing. Every number below was measured in a browser, not guessed.

← back to the preview

Two things worth fixing in Plain as it stands

1
Muted text misses AA by a hair. --ink-3: #777 on white is 4.48:1, just under the 4.5:1 floor.[6] That colour is every date, every caption, the footer and the photo counts. Each variant lifts it to 5.1 or better.
2
Lines run long. 620px of Verdana at 14.5px measures 82 characters. The band with the best comprehension evidence is roughly 45–75.[2] Every variant lands on 68.

Two more things I changed everywhere, quietly. Sizes moved from px to rem, so someone who has raised their browser's default font size actually gets it — px ignores that setting, zoom notwithstanding. And the measure is expressed in em computed from each face's real average character width, not ch: ch is the width of a zero, which runs about 35% wider than average prose, so 66ch renders as 86–92 characters, not 66.

What I did not touch

Verdana at 14.5px is not as small as it looks. Its x-height is 0.545em, so it sets a 7.9px x-height — the same physical letter size as Arial at 15.3px or Georgia at 16.4px. Reading speed holds flat across a wide range of print sizes as long as you stay above the critical size,[1] and Plain is above it. So the sizes move in the variants for voice and for the measure, not because 14.5px was failing anyone.

Links stay underlined everywhere. None of these link colours reach 3:1 against the body ink, which would be the requirement if colour were the only thing marking a link.[6] The browser's default underline is doing real work; only the nav and the site name drop it. Visited links keep a distinct colour in all five — it is one of the oldest and most reliably useful navigation cues on the web.[9]

Reader

built for the long posts
Georgia1.06rem1.6568 char measureno font download

Warm-neutral paper, deep indigo links, a serif at 17px. The dates and captions move to the mono face because Georgia's figures are old-style — lovely inside a sentence, hopeless in a column that wants to line up.

EvidenceScreen studies keep failing to find a reliable reading-speed difference between serif and sans,[4] so the face is a voice decision, not a legibility one. What does move the needle is measure: 55 characters gave the best comprehension in Dyson & Haselgrove, and long lines read faster but understood worse.[2] Georgia was drawn by Matthew Carter for screens and ships on effectively every machine, so it costs nothing to load.
CostA serif at a small size needs the size bump to hold together, which makes the page taller. If you dislike serifs on a personal site, this one is dead on arrival regardless of the numbers.

Hyperlegible

accessibility taken seriously
Atkinson Hyperlegible1.03rem1.768 char measure~40KB webfont

The Braille Institute's face, drawn so that characters people confuse — capital I, lowercase l, digit 1, O and 0 — stay apart from each other. Slashed zero, tailed l, asymmetric b/d. Stronger rules, a 3px focus ring, 32px nav targets, roomier list spacing.

EvidenceLetterform disambiguation is the design brief the face was tested against for low-vision readers.[8] The 1.7 line height clears WCAG's text-spacing floor of 1.5 with room to spare,[6] the focus ring meets the 2.4.13 focus-appearance shape, and 32px nav targets clear the 24px minimum in 2.5.8. Every colour here is 5:1 or better in both modes.
CostA real font download and a flash of fallback text unless you preload it. The face is also opinionated — it looks like an accessibility font, which is either the point or a dealbreaker.

Console

nothing to download
system-ui1rem1.668 char measureno font download

Cool slate ground with white cards, and the mono face for everything the machine generated: nav, dates, picture counts, year headings, captions. Prose stays in the system face, which on your Mac is SF, on Windows Segoe, on Android Roboto.

EvidenceZero font bytes means no FOIT, no swap-in reflow, and nothing on the critical path — font loading is one of the few typography choices that shows up directly in Core Web Vitals. The system face is also the one the OS hints and renders best. Tabular figures in the date column genuinely line up.
CostSplitting prose and metadata into two faces is a craft call, not a research finding — I am not going to pretend otherwise. And your site looks slightly different on every OS, which is either honest or sloppy depending on your mood.

Nightshift

dark designed first
Public Sans1rem1.6268 char measurevariable webfont

The dark palette was drawn first and the light one derived from it, rather than the usual inversion. Surfaces are #121317 rather than black, accents are desaturated, cards lose their borders and separate by elevation instead, and the body weight drops from 400 to 350 in the dark.

EvidenceDark-on-light still wins for most sighted readers in normal light,[5] which is why the light mode here is not an afterthought. But dark mode has its own failure modes: light text on pure black blooms, and saturated accents vibrate against dark grounds — both are why Material specifies a near-black surface and desaturated colour.[10] WCAG 2's contrast maths is optimistic at the dark end,[7] so I checked every colour against APCA as well: the muted greys in Plain's dark mode pass WCAG at 6.4:1 but land at Lc 47, under the ~60 floor for small text. Nightshift's sit at 63.
CostA variable font download. And the weight drop is a preference of mine dressed in a plausible story about optical bloom — I have no study for it. Turn it off by deleting one line.

Where the evidence is thin

Worth saying plainly, since the point of doing this from research is not pretending everything has a citation.

  • Warm off-white reduces eye strain. Repeated everywhere, evidenced almost nowhere. Reader's paper is warm because it suits a serif, not because it is easier on your eyes.
  • Pure black backgrounds hurt. Halation is real for astigmatism and some low-vision readers, but the blanket claim is practitioner lore. The near-black surface in Nightshift is cheap insurance, not a proven fix.
  • Dyslexia-specific fonts. Studies that tested them found no advantage over ordinary good sans faces; what did help was larger text and shorter lines.[3] That is why none of these use one.
  • Preference is not performance. Several of these studies found readers preferred settings that did not measurably help them. Pick the one you like looking at — the numbers above only rule out the ones that would quietly cost someone else.

References

  1. Legge & Bigelow (2011). Does print size matter for reading? A review of findings from vision science and typography. Journal of Vision 11(5):8. Reading speed is flat across a wide range of print sizes above a critical size; x-height, not point size, is the meaningful measure.
  2. Dyson & Haselgrove (2001). The influence of reading speed and line length on the effectiveness of reading from screen. International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 54(4). 55 characters per line gave the best comprehension; 100 characters read faster but was understood less well.
  3. Rello & Baeza-Yates (2013, 2016). Good fonts for dyslexia (W4A) and The effect of font type on screen readability by people with dyslexia (ACM TACCESS). Larger sizes helped; sans, roman and monospaced faces outperformed italics and some serifs; dyslexia-specific faces did not win.
  4. Poole (2008) review of serif vs sans research, and Arditi & Cho (2005), Vision Research. No reliable serif/sans difference in screen reading speed.
  5. Buchner & Baumgartner (2007), Ergonomics; Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner (2013/2014). Positive polarity (dark text on light) advantage in proofreading and acuity for normal vision under typical lighting, linked to pupil size.
  6. WCAG 2.2. 1.4.1 use of colour, 1.4.3 contrast 4.5:1, 1.4.12 text spacing (line height at least 1.5), 2.4.13 focus appearance, 2.5.8 target size 24×24. Technique G183 covers the 3:1 rule for links distinguished by colour alone.
  7. APCA / Somers, the contrast model drafted for WCAG 3. WCAG 2's ratio consistently overstates legibility on dark backgrounds; Lc 75 is the working minimum for body text, Lc 90 preferred.
  8. Braille Institute, Atkinson Hyperlegible. Designed around character disambiguation for low-vision readers.
  9. Nielsen Norman Group, long-running guidance on visited-link colour and underlined links as the most recognised link affordance.
  10. Material Design dark theme guidance. #121212-class surfaces rather than black, desaturated accents, elevation over borders.
